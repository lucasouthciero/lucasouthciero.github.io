Problem

Two linked questions about network risk. First, how does a disruption actually travel through a supply chain, and where should a company hold buffer stock? Second, in a fictional course case, how should Woodson Chemical Company (WCC) fix a fragmented, division-siloed supply chain that was driving poor customer service and rising costs?

Approach

Part 1: Ripple effect simulation

  • Separated the ripple effect (structural, low-frequency, high-impact disruptions) from the bullwhip effect (operational, high-frequency demand and lead time swings).
  • Found that how far a disruption propagates, not how long it lasts, was the strongest predictor of a service-level failure.
  • Compared safety stock strategies. A mixed allocation, high upstream and lean downstream, preserved 98%+ service levels while cutting holding costs.
  • Recommended positioning safety stock downstream of high-risk nodes rather than at suppliers.

Part 2: Woodson Chemical (WCC) case

  • Analyzed WCC's division-siloed information systems: 1,500+ SKUs, 22 plants, 5 DCs and 325 stocking points.
  • Recommended a hybrid-centralized ERP and data warehouse architecture, unifying divisional data while preserving local execution flexibility.
  • Built the cost case: sales and admin expenses grew more than 40% against 13% sales growth from 2015 to 2019, which justified moving away from redundant divisional software.
  • Proposed freight telemetry (IoT tracking) feeding a customer-facing portal for real-time order visibility.
  • Weighed risk and benefit tradeoffs for corporate, line distribution and customer stakeholders before recommending implementation.

Result

A simulation-backed rule for where to hold safety stock, and a hybrid-centralized ERP recommendation for WCC supported by its own financials and a stakeholder tradeoff analysis.

What I learned

With a product footprint this large and satisfaction scores this low, the pull to fix everything at once was strong. I ended up over-promising a solution without fully accounting for the true scale of implementation. It was a reminder that recommendations need to match what an organization can realistically absorb.
